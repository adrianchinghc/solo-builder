# Indie Maker Philosophy

## The Core Belief

The Silicon Valley narrative is broken: raise VC → hire a big team → build in secret → launch → hope. Pieter Levels built Nomads.com, Remote OK, and Hoodmaps to $3M+/year in revenue, alone, working from hotels and cafes, while VC-funded teams of 30+ people building the same products failed. This is the alternative playbook.

The indie maker approach: **build something yourself, see if it works, make money, repeat.** No investors. No office. No permission. Just ship.

---

## Solve Your Own Problems First

The single most important idea in this entire philosophy: **find ideas by solving problems you actually have.**

You are the greatest expert at your own problems. When Pieter built Nomads.com, he was a traveling nomad trying to figure out which cities had fast internet and low cost of living. He wasn't researching a market. He built a spreadsheet for himself. It accidentally became a business.

**Why this beats market research:**
- You already understand the problem deeply — you don't need to interview users
- You'll be your own first user and catch real issues immediately
- You evolve with the problem as your life evolves
- The founder of Boosted Boards built electric skateboards because he wanted one. He knew exactly how it should feel.

**The flip side:** If you keep coming up with the same ideas everyone else has (todo apps, photo sharing, maps of friends), you're not original enough. The fix isn't to research better ideas — **it's to live a more original life.** Go places. Do things that make you different. Your unusual experiences become unusual product ideas.

---

## Ideas Are Worthless Without Execution

> "To me, ideas are worth nothing unless executed. They are just a multiplier. Execution is worth millions."
> — Derek Sivers

Don't protect your idea. Share it freely. If someone "steals" it, they'll execute it differently — 10 people with the same idea build 10 different companies. Your execution is what matters.

**Don't be afraid to share ideas because:**
- It forces you to articulate the value (makes the idea clearer)
- Other people's feedback evolves the idea
- If no one gets excited, that's early signal the idea needs work

The real validation isn't what friends say — it's whether the market pays for it. No one knows if an idea works until you launch it.

---

## Start Small, Become Big

**Never start by trying to build the big thing.**

Every major company started tiny:
- Facebook was a "Hot or Not" clone
- Apple was a computer kit for amateur hackers
- Google was a search tool for Stanford's intranet
- Nomads.com was a spreadsheet of 25 cities

The pattern: **micro-niche → multi-niche → adjacent markets → platform**

Example:
1. Booking software for hairdressers who do African hair (10,000 potential customers)
2. All hairdressers (100,000+)
3. Nail salons, tattoo shops
4. All local service businesses
5. Add payments → now you're a payment platform

Starting small is not a limitation. It's how you find the path of least resistance. Big companies start big and crash. Small companies start small, find what works, and grow naturally.

**The math:** 1,000 people paying $83/month = $1,000,000/year. You don't need a mass market. You need a small, loyal niche.

---

## Build Fast, Ship Now, Perfect Later

Your enemy is perfectionism. Not bugs. Not competition. **Perfectionism.**

Perfectionism causes inaction. 50 meetings. 100 iterations. Endless redesigns. And still no product in anyone's hands. The user decides if your product is good, not you.

**Minimum viable product ≠ minimum effort.** It means minimum scope, but the core has to actually work. Don't ship a landing page with no product. Ship something that does one thing well, even if everything else is rough.

Rules for shipping fast:
- Ship when the core works, not when everything is perfect
- Bugs are fine if users can report them and you fix them fast
- Wrong colors and images are fine for a day
- Users accept rough if the core solves their problem
- You can always iterate — but only if you launch first

> Gmail was in beta for a decade. Users are more patient than you think.

---

## DIY Beats Outsourcing (At the Start)

Pieter competed against VC-funded teams of 30+ people and beat them. From a hotel room. Working in his underwear.

Why? Because he could fix a bug in 5 minutes over morning coffee. The VC team had to email a developer, schedule a meeting, wait for approval, then wait for the developer to actually do it. Days later, maybe.

**The compounding advantage:** 1,000 small improvements per year × faster cycle time = product that's orders of magnitude better than what a slow team ships.

**If you can't code yet:**
- Use no-code tools to validate the idea first
- Learn by doing: Google every problem, don't take courses
- Don't learn an entire language — learn what you need for today's problem

## The No-Code MVP Stack

You can build a real, paying product without writing a single line of code:

| What you need | Tool |
|---|---|
| Landing page | Carrd, Squarespace, Tilda |
| Collect user data / forms | Typeform, Google Forms |
| Process and route data | Zapier |
| Email users | MailChimp |
| Accept payments | Stripe (via Carrd or Typeform) |
| Task management for contractors | Trello |
| SMS notifications | Twilio (via Zapier) |

This stack can run a fully functional business. Once a no-code version is validated and making money, you can replace pieces with custom code. But many founders never need to.

## Bootstrapping vs. Venture Capital

**VC startup path:**
- Get funded → hire team → rent office → build something → run out of money → shut down

**Bootstrapped path:**
- Build something → see if it works → make money → hire if needed → sell if you want

Bootstrapping advantages:
- **Full ownership**: when you sell, you keep most of the money
- **Low cost**: no team salaries eating your margin before you have revenue
- **No growth pressure**: you don't need to 10x to return the fund, you just need to profit
- **Less stress**: your goal is ramen profitability, not billion-dollar exit
- **Speed**: one person who knows the whole codebase moves faster than a team with meetings

The startup press tells you you need VC. But Pieter's Nomads.com, Plenty of Fish ($575M exit — built by one person), and Basecamp ($168M revenue) were all bootstrapped. Revenue-generating bootstrapped businesses attract investors when they actually need them — but by then, you don't.

---

## Constraints Are Advantages

**No money:** Bootstrapping forces you to keep costs low and find revenue fast. This is healthy pressure. Companies that can't survive on their own revenue often shouldn't exist yet.

**No office:** Access to worldwide talent. No rent. Forces remote-first culture from day one.

**No coding skills:** You're forced to use off-the-shelf tools and ship an MVP quickly. Less architecture debt. More focus on whether the problem is real.

**No connections:** You're an underdog. People love to root for underdogs. The "scene" creates groupthink — you think more freely outside it.

## The Portfolio Approach: Keep Shipping

Pieter ran 12 startups in 12 months. Most failed. A few succeeded enormously. This is not a failure rate — it's a strategy.

You will probably need to ship 10–30 products over 1–3 years before something breaks through. The ones who succeed aren't smarter — they just shipped more and failed faster.

**Rules for running multiple projects:**
- Your best projects get 80% of your time, secondary ones 20%
- If something isn't getting traction: kill it fast, don't waste years on it
- If something is working: don't abandon it for the next shiny idea
- Log every idea (Workflowy or Trello): Concepts → Promising → Building → Success/Failure
- Write a one-sentence post-mortem for every failure

**How to keep motivation:**
- If users are engaged, keep building
- If nobody is using it after real effort: kill it
- Money is the clearest signal — if people pay, the idea is real

---

## "Always Keep Shipping" 🚢

The single rule that ties everything together. The biggest risk isn't building the wrong thing. It's building nothing at all.

Stagnancy kills. Action — even imperfect action — is how you find what works. Ship something this week. You can fix it next week.
