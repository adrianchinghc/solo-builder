# Exit & Acquisition Playbook

## The Reality of Exits

Most founders who bootstrap to $100K–$500K+ ARR will eventually receive acquisition inquiries. Pieter Levels received tens of offers over 4 years. He proceeded to serious negotiations once — a multi-million dollar offer that he ultimately rejected over bad terms. His lesson: **90–99% of acquisition messages are a waste of time.** The few that aren't can change your life. Knowing the difference early saves months of stress.

You don't need VC to exit well. Plenty of Fish sold for $575M bootstrapped. Basecamp — self-funded — is estimated at $168M+ revenue. The exit path for indie makers is real.

---

## Don't Put Yourself for Sale

The best negotiating position is **not needing or wanting to sell.** If you're profitable, growing, and your business is running well, you can price aggressively high because walking away is fine. This mental posture — "I'll sell if the price is right, not because I need to" — gives you leverage that no negotiation tactic can replicate.

Never:
- List your company on marketplaces when you're desperate
- Tell buyers you need to sell
- Set artificial deadlines that pressure you

## The 4 Types of Buyers

### 1. Deadbeat (90–95% of inquiries)
**Signs:** They contact you with an offer that's 100–1,000x below market rate. Someone offering $5,000 for a $300K ARR business is a deadbeat.
**Action:** Ignore. Don't reply. Your time is worth more than this conversation.

### 2. Competitive Snooper (3–5% of inquiries)
**Signs:** They seem interested and serious but are actually competitors extracting intelligence. They'll walk you through detailed due diligence — asking for revenue breakdowns, customer lists, tech architecture, growth strategies — then back out at the final stage.
**Signs you're being snooped:**
- The buyer is a direct competitor or in an adjacent market
- They're unusually interested in your customer acquisition channels
- They keep asking for more data before committing to any price
- The process drags on with no clear timeline

**What competitive snooping costs you:** months of work, stress, distraction from your real business, and the sharing of proprietary data that can be used against you.

**Defense: Require a Breakup Fee**

Before sharing *any* confidential information, require the buyer to agree to a breakup fee — a cash penalty they pay if they walk away after the due diligence phase. This:
- Filters non-serious buyers immediately (they'll refuse or disappear)
- Compensates you for the intelligence you do share
- Keeps the serious buyer committed to seeing the deal through

Industry standard is 1–3% of the deal price. Pieter recommends being more aggressive (10%) for bootstrapped deals. Big companies do this routinely — AT&T paid a $4B breakup fee to T-Mobile.

### 3. String-You-Alonger (2–3% of inquiries)
**Signs:** They schedule a call, then reschedule it. Then reschedule again. "Let's chat next month." This continues indefinitely.
**Why they do it:** Middle managers at large companies who don't have decision-making authority but enjoy the theater of "strategic initiatives."
**Defense:** Decline all calls. Require **text-only communication.** Their whole performance collapses in text — they can't bullshit as easily in writing.

### 4. Serious Buyer (~1% of inquiries)
**Signs:** Concise, direct messages. They know what they want. They make a real offer early. The process is simple and in your favor. They're friendly and show genuine trust.
**Big-tech buyers** (Google, Stripe, etc.) can be informal and efficient when they want something — don't assume "big company = bureaucracy."
**Action:** Engage. These are the only ones worth your time.

## Setting Up for Inbound Offers

Don't list your business for sale. Let buyers come to you. This keeps you in power.

**How to attract serious buyers passively:**
- Keep your email public (on your about page)
- Keep DMs open on Twitter/X
- Publish your revenue numbers (like Pieter does with his open page)
- Build a brand around yourself — buyers want the product AND the story
- If you're profitable and visible, serious buyers will find you

## Negotiating the Price

### Let Them Bid First
Don't open with a price. Ask the buyer to make a first offer. This:
- Filters the deadbeats (their number reveals their seriousness)
- Avoids anchoring too low
- Lets you understand their framing and how badly they want it

If they push back ("we need to know your asking price"), say: "I'd love to understand your range first to see if we're in the same ballpark."

### If You Open: Anchor Aggressively High

If you do choose to name a price first, open at **10x annual revenue.** Research supports this — the first number anchors the negotiation, and most founders anchor far too low.

Example:
```
Your business makes $200K/year.
Industry standard: 2–4x revenue = $400K–$800K
Your opening price: 10x = $2,000,000
Final negotiated price: likely $800K–$1.2M
```

An aggressive first offer doesn't scare away serious buyers. It does scare away non-serious ones — which is exactly what you want.

### Standard Valuation Multiples

| Business type | Typical multiple |
|---|---|
| Bootstrapped SaaS, stable MRR | 3–5x annual revenue |
| High-growth SaaS | 5–10x annual revenue |
| Content site / niche media | 2–4x annual revenue |
| Job board / marketplace | 3–5x annual revenue |

The multiple depends on: growth rate, churn rate, owner-dependence, automation level, and traffic source diversity. A highly automated business with diversified traffic (not dependent on one channel) commands a higher multiple.

## Deal Terms to Watch

### Cash vs. Stock
**Always prefer cash.** Stock in the acquiring company is illiquid, volatile, and may be worth nothing. If they offer stock, either reject it or price it at a steep discount to cash.

### Earnout Bonuses — The Trap
An earnout means part of your payment is contingent on hitting future performance targets AFTER the sale. Example: $500K now + $300K if you hit $X ARR in 2 years under their ownership.

**Avoid earnouts.** Once you've sold, you lose control of the product, the team, and the strategy. The very things that made it successful may be changed by the new owner. Then they blame you for missing targets.

If they insist on earnout, demand it be structured as a bonus (they pay you the earnout regardless if they change the product) rather than contingent performance.

### Retention / Earn-In
Buyers often want you to stay on for 6–24 months post-acquisition to ensure knowledge transfer. This is usually fine. Negotiate: how many hours/week, what your role is, and what happens if the relationship sours.

## Using a Broker

**Benefits:**
- They have a buyer network already
- They know how to present your business attractively
- They handle negotiation and legal logistics
- They get you a fair market price (they do this regularly, you don't)

**Downsides:**
- 10–15% of the sale price goes to the broker. On $1M, that's $150K.
- Short-term mindset — brokers want to close deals to earn commissions
- Less leverage in negotiation (they may push you to accept lower offers to close faster)

**Best brokers for indie/bootstrapped businesses:**
- **FE International (FEI)** — most reputable for online businesses
- **Empire Flippers** — good for content sites, SaaS, e-commerce
- **Acquire.com** — self-serve marketplace for smaller deals (<$5M)

Use a broker if you've never sold a business before and the deal size is significant. Skip a broker if you're confident negotiating and have legal support.

## Due Diligence — What Buyers Will Ask For

Once a serious buyer is confirmed (breakup fee signed), they'll request:
- 12–24 months of revenue and MRR data
- Traffic analytics (Google Analytics access)
- Customer churn rate and LTV
- Tech stack documentation
- Server costs and infrastructure
- Any outstanding legal issues

**Prep this now, before you get offers.** A clean, organized data room closes deals faster and signals professionalism. Disorganized books kill deals.

## The Emotional Reality

Pieter spent 6 months in serious negotiations and rejected the deal. What he learned: **the moment you think you're about to receive a life-changing sum of money, your judgment gets clouded.** You'll want to say yes to things you shouldn't. The other side knows this.

**Rules for emotional stability:**
- Remind yourself daily that you don't need to sell
- Talk to at least one founder who has sold a company before
- Have a lawyer review every agreement before you sign
- Build in "sleep on it" pauses before major decisions
- Have a clear walk-away number before negotiations start — and stick to it

---

## Do This Today

Write down the number at which you would sell your current product today with no hesitation. Then write the number at which you would need to think about it for a week. The difference between those two numbers is your negotiation room. Keep both private.
